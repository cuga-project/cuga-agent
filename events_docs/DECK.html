Channels & Events: where we are

Two features, built side by side on top of CUGA core. Channels let people talk to CUGA from Slack, Discord, Telegram or the web. Events let them ask CUGA to keep doing something — on a schedule, on a change, or on an inbound webhook — with a human approving the exact prompt before anything is armed.

The two features

Channels first, then events on top

They are deliberately separate. Channels is a transport question — how does a message reach the agent and how does the answer get back. Events is a time question — what happens when nobody is in the room.

① Channels — talk to CUGA from anywhere

Four surfaces, all live: web, Slack, Discord, Telegram. Each normalises into one envelope and posts to /invoke. Slack and Discord require an @mention to engage; once the bot roots a thread, follow-ups in that thread need no re-mention. Delivery goes back to the same thread it came from.

② Events — make it keep happening

A plain chat stays plain chat. Only /automate, /watch, /schedule reach the eventing layer. It arms a cron, a poll (fire only when something changed) or an inbound webhook — then fires on its own and delivers back through whichever channel you came from.

The round trip, concretely

1 · in
You @bot /automate every minute send me the price of bitcoin in Slack.
2 · normalise
The Slack watcher turns it into the envelope and POSTs /invoke.
3 · arm?
Concierge sees a slash verb → builds a draft, does not arm.
4 · confirm
You get a card in that thread: the exact prompt, the cadence, the delivery target. Reply yes — no re-mention needed.
5 · armed
Subscription stored with the origin thread on it.
6 · fire
Native scheduler wakes it. Each fire runs on a fresh thread so turn #288 doesn't drag 287 prior turns.
7 · execute
Supervisor picks the specialist (pricebot) and calls its MCP tools.
8 · back
Answer delivered into the original Slack thread. Execution thread ≠ delivery thread.

NL → Flow

Nothing arms until you approve the prompt

The risky part of "turn this sentence into a standing job" is not the schedule — it's the prompt the agent will be handed every single fire, forever, with nobody watching. So we put a human in front of it.

  1. DRAFT — the utterance is parsed into cadence + prompt + delivery target. The scaffolding ("every 5 minutes", "send me") is stripped out of the prompt, because the schedule already handles recurrence.
  2. NEEDS_INPUT — if something required is missing (no cadence, no push source), the concierge asks. It talks back through the same channel you came from, and waits.
  3. CONFIRM — you see a card: this exact prompt, this cadence, delivered here. Three choices: Arm it · Edit prompt (prefilled, change it, it re-confirms) · Cancel.
  4. ARMED — and only then. Cron and poll arm deterministically from the approved text; there is no second LLM pass free to arm something you didn't read.

Drafts expire after 10 minutes. A plain chat message never enters this dialogue at all.

Deployment

One process, or two — same wire contract

Combined default

Events routes mount onto CUGA's FastAPI. One process, one image, one port. This is what to reach for.

Split verified

Two services: vanilla CUGA (cuga-core) and the eventing service (cuga-events-svc), which executes by calling /run over HTTP. Same routes, same shapes — every harness and every armed subscription works unchanged.

Why the split matters: CUGA and the eventing layer get to scale, deploy and hold secrets separately — cuga-core carries no bot tokens, no scheduler, no channel loops.

The rule that took us three passes to learn: the roster belongs to whoever executes. In the split that's CUGA, so CUGA publishes what it has loaded at GET /run/agents and the events side asks instead of guessing.

Activepieces

Wired in, switched off — and why we still care

What works without it

Everything you've seen: all four chat channels, cron, poll, inbound webhooks, and the direct watchers for Slack, Discord and Box. The native scheduler runs cron/poll in-process. No extra infra, no container to babysit.

What it unlocks when you turn it on

Push triggers on SaaS you don't own — Gmail new-email, GitHub repo events, Box new-file, Calendar. AP holds the OAuth tokens (encrypted; the agent never sees them) and does the provider-specific webhook plumbing we would otherwise write ~30 times.

Why it's off: it's a whole extra service, and every path that doesn't need it should not depend on it. Keeping it optional is a design position — AP is one trigger backend, not the spine. The harness proves it: with AP down, the only failures are the two assertions that exist purely to check AP is down.

Heads up for the day you enable it: we fixed a latent crash in the AP engine this session — reachable() had been pasted into the middle of __init__, so half the constructor never ran. It fails on the first AP call with 'APEngine' object has no attribute '_auth_lock'. Invisible while AP is off.

# sanity: you should see 7 tool servers and an 8-agent roster

By hand — the part worth seeing

Open http://localhost:7860/studio → Concierge, and type:

/automate every 5 minutes send IBM stock price

You get a confirm card, not an armed flow. Try all three buttons. Then do the same in Slack with @your-bot /automate every minute send me the price of bitcoin and reply yes in the thread — a minute later the tick lands in that same thread.
